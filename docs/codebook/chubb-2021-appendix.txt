Appendix

Maritime Assertiveness Time Series Dataset:

PRC in the South China Sea, 1970-2015

This article’s time series of assertive changes in the PRC’s behavior in
the South China Sea dispute between 1970 and 2015 is the first dataset
in a larger Maritime Assertiveness Time Series (MATS) project. As of
March 2020, data collection is complete for Vietnam and the Philippines’
assertive behavior in the South China Sea across the same time period;
planned future rounds of data collection will include the PRC and Japan
in the East China Sea, and the PRC and India in their terrestrial border
dispute.

Preliminary data on assertive changes in the PRC’s behavior in the South
China Sea dispute between 1970 and 2015 were compiled from publicly
available materials in English- and Chinese-language, as well as some
Vietnamese-language sources accessed through Google Translate. However,
as noted, a present-centric bias exists in the information supply on
such events due to recent increases in attention towards the South China
Sea issue, as well as greater publicity from the PRC regarding its own
activities. This makes assertive behavior more likely to be observed in
recent years. To counteract this, the dataset used here draws
extensively upon historical PRC sources. These include
internal-circulation PRC chronologies on major events in the South China
Sea covering the period up to 1996 (Zhang, NSQDDSJ), advisory reports on
the South China Sea from 2002 to 2009 (NISCSS, NHXSPGBG), archived PRC
maritime agency web pages (SOA, “Dashiji”), reports on state-run
scientific expeditions in the 1980s (CAS, Nansha Qundao), and yearbooks
of PRC government maritime agencies covering the 1970s through to
present (SOA, ZGHYNJ; Fisheries, ZGYYNJ). Other publicly available
materials used included one declassified US military reports and several
leaked State Department cables. A full list is appended in the data
file.

Stage 1: Master timeline

Data collection took place via a two-stage process. The first was the
generation of a master timeline file (not restricted to the post-1970
era) into which was entered any potentially relevant historical
developments in the South China Sea dispute, as encountered by the
author in the course of researching the issue. The sources for this base
of raw information on the dispute included existing academic literature
and policy analysis, open-source reports by foreign government
departments, archived press reporting accessed through the Factiva
database, and news on contemporary events acquired through English and
Chinese language media, usually online. The content of the master
timeline was not limited to PRC actions and those of its rivals, but
included any plausibly relevant information on developments in the
dispute: reported actions of third countries, notable statements by
officials, bilateral meetings and agreements, important domestic
political events, and exogenous international developments. The aim in
this process was to cast the net as widely as possible, leaving analysis
and verification to the second stage.

The timeline has been continuously added to, filled in, corrected and
updated throughout the seven-year duration (as of early 2020) of the
project, as more and better information on came to hand. A major step in
this process was the systematic study of several important PRC sources
mentioned above. Besides identifying numerous otherwise unreported
events, this process clarified the timing and nature (and in some cases
existence) of already-reported events, while adding valuable inside-out
perspective on how the dispute’s progression appeared from within
China’s party-state system. The master timeline therefore serves as a
reference resource on the context of China’s actions in the South China
Sea. It also provides the basis for a more refined set of data with
which to identify the timing and nature of the recent changes in the
state’s behavior.

Stage 2: Refined time series

The second stage involved refining content from the master timeline by
applying the concept of assertiveness outlined in the main text and
coding each event that met the definition qualitatively within the
four-way typology. The resultant time series captures the variation in
China’s assertiveness between 1970 and 2015, relative to 1970. The
appropriateness of this reference point is discussed below. Each case
constituted an intensification of the PRC’s assertiveness in comparison
with the previous year. In total, 132 instances of assertive change,
year-on-year, in PRC’s conduct were identified. Per the conceptual
framework established in the main text of the article, three criteria
determined whether a given action constituted an intensification of
assertiveness: qualitative novelty, increased frequency or expanded
geographic scope. Using the previous calendar year as reference point
served this project’s requirement for fairly precise indications of the
timing of policy change. The limitations of this method are discussed
separately below.

Verification of cases

Although it was not possible to achieve complete certainty regarding the
details of every case, each was cross-checked with other sources where
available, and considered with specific regard to possible motivations
that could bias the source of the information. Several candidate cases
from the main timeline were set aside pending possible future
confirmation (see data file).

The background to each candidate case of intensified assertive behavior
demanded careful consideration. Various aspects of the “status quo” in
disputed maritime areas change constantly, such as numbers of ships,
patterns of navigation, personnel and equipment. Where a significantly
new situation arises, the question of whether a particular state action
constitutes an assertive change in behavior hinges on a counterfactual
judgment as to how the state would have responded if the same
circumstances had arisen in the past.[1] To take a concrete example, PRC
on-water enforcement actions against foreign fishing increased in 2007,
but this could not automatically be considered a case of intensified
assertiveness. The number of Vietnamese fishers operating in the Paracel
Islands had increased sharply, and if this had occurred in previous
years it is quite likely that PRC enforcement would also have
quantitatively increased. The case’s eventual inclusion in the dataset
was on the qualitative basis that China’s enforcement methods shifted
from the previous practice of harassment and warnings, towards
confiscation of property and detention of Vietnamese personnel.

The next step was to consider the likely duration of each action, or
pattern of action, in order to identify whether it continued,
intensified further or diminished in subsequent years. In most
instances, an absence of evidence of equivalent actions in the following
year was treated as a diminishment of that particular aspect of China’s
assertiveness. There were exceptions, however particularly where long
periods separated available data points. One example concerned PRC
fishing. Chinese, Vietnamese and Philippine sources reported an increase
in PRC fishing activities in disputed parts of the South China Sea in
1994, and the China Fisheries Yearbook records a 26 percent rise in PRC
state-sanctioned fishing trips to the Spratly Islands in 2001, but the
intensity of China’s fishing activities in the intervening years was
unknown. In this case the assumption was made that Chinese fishing had
been occurring at approximately the same level across that time period –
a more cautious conclusion than to assume either further increases or
diminishment. PRC fishing was therefore regarded as having intensified
in 1994, and continued over the following seven years, before
intensifying again in 2001.

Limitations

These events data are inevitably incomplete, for an assertive state and
its adversaries may both have incentives to keep such events secret. To
mitigate this risk of unobserved events the author consulted historical
sources from different contending sides of the dispute, and concentrated
special attention to uncovering events from the period before the
dispute became subject to widespread English-language media and
scholarly attention, over a period of six years (2011-2017). In
recognition of the fact that events in the distant past are both harder
to verify and less likely to be found in the first place, stricter
evidentiary standards were applied for more recent events.
Methodologically, this is appropriate for a study seeking to identify
and explain change, for it reduces the probability that changes detected
will be spurious or overstated. It should also increase analytic
confidence in the existence of any recent changes that are identified.

As with any attempt to distil social processes to numbers, it is crucial
to bear in mind the relevant context that may be obscured by these
methods. First, these data only include non-cooperative aspects of
China’s behaviour. The assertiveness framework does capture moderations
of behavior — that is, shifts to fewer, or qualitatively less
escalatory, assertive actions over a given time period. But it does not
account for actively conciliatory actions such as agreements over
resource exploitation or management, joint patrols and other
confidence-building measures (CBMs). While it would be desirable to
capture all aspects of a state’s dispute behavior in one dataset, the
exclusion of cooperative actions was necessary because actively
conciliatory measures do not necessarily offset or mitigate assertive
actions.[2] This decision also reflects the project’s purpose of
identifying the timing and nature of the recent change in state
behaviour in the South China Sea in order to better understand its
causes. Future work may develop appropriate ways to simultaneously
account for both non-cooperative and cooperative dispute behaviour.

Second, the events data are limited to those actions that are observable
and documented in the sources consulted. The strategy employed to
counteract this consisted of (1) casting the data-gathering net as
widely as possible, including both English-and Chinese-language sources,
and (2) including all possibly relevant developments, both confirmed and
unconfirmed, in the master timeline from which the events data were
drawn, thus allowing time for evidence to emerge that could confirm or
refute candidate cases. Another related limitation inherent in relying
on events data is that there may be a lag between important political
decisions that set in motion a policy change, and the observable
manifestations of this. Thus, positivist approach that underpin these
data need to be complemented by close analysis of speech and text, in
order to account for how the actors involved understood their actions,
as well as to identify more remote but nonetheless fundamental causes of
policy change.

Third, being focused on China’s assertiveness, these data do not account
for other states’ assertive actions. Instead, these are discussed
separately in the process of explaining the observed changes in PRC
behavior. External developments, for example, relate closely to the
“declining claim strength” explanation. As noted above, external
developments were included in the master timeline, but not the 132 cases
of PRC policy change that constitute the time series analyzed here.
Although beyond the scope of this article’s analysis, the MATS project
has so far gathered data matching time series on the assertive actions
of the Philippines and Vietnam. Analysis of these and other matched time
series capturing behavioral changes of other claimants and powers that
operate in the area, such as the U.S., Australia, Japan and India, could
offer new insight into the action-reaction dynamics of the
Asia-Pacific’s disputed maritime spaces.[3] Further planned expansion of
the MATS project will encompass include territorial disputes, starting
with the PRC-India border dispute.

Unit of analysis

Measuring behavioral change by comparing state actions in one calendar
year with the one that preceded it was a pragmatic decision. Other units
of comparison could generate different findings about when, or even
whether, change has occurred. The specific actions taken in the
Impeccable incident, for example, were probably unprecedented, but the
incident can also be understood as a continuation of the policy that
produced the EP-3 incident in a similar area in 2001. Similarly, the
unilateral resource survey activities China carried out in 2014 are not
known to have occurred in 2013, but similar activities were observed in
similar geographical areas in 2010 and 2007. Although this could be
characterized this as a single case of change (no explorations before
2007, explorations every 3-4 years afterwards), under the year-on-year
method this constitutes three separate cases of intensification of
assertive activity, followed by its diminishment the following year.
Besides allowing for the identification of the timing of behavioural
change down to a one-year range, using calendar years also seems a less
arbitrary choice than wider units of time.

The quantitative assertiveness framework implicitly gives equal weight
to actions of varying significance. For example, the method of handling
Vietnamese fishers in the Paracels became more coercive in 2009 and 2011
with the increased detentions of personnel and seizure of boats, but its
intensity remained far below that seen in the January 2005
Sino-Vietnamese fisheries incident in the Gulf of Tonkin, in which nine
Vietnamese personnel were killed, or the fatal 2007 incident in the
Paracels. South China Sea sovereignty declarations in the party’s
official press increased substantially 2009 and 2011, but the party
media had been equally vociferous in 1999 and 2004, and much more vocal
in 1988 and 1995. The massive land reclamation works since late 2013 are
also not weighted for their significance, except insofar as they enable
further assertive actions in the future. The typology used here
represents a compromise between the need for a more systematic
measurement of change, and the need to account for the significance of
different kinds of actions.

Baseline 1970

The year 1970 is a logical starting point because it follows closely
after preliminary surveys suggested the possible presence of major
resource deposits in the area. This “discovery” created one of the key
factors in the high-stakes dispute that exists today. Although several
states, including the PRC, announced claims in the area in the 1940s and
1950s, the UN Economic Commission for Asia and the Far East’s survey
indicating potentially massive oil and gas bounties sharply increased
the incentives for claimants on all sides to pursue those claims.[4] The
list below shows a selection of known instances of PRC actions over the
South China Sea between 1949 and 1969 that provides the zero-baseline
for comparing the PRC’s assertiveness between 1970 and 2015.

Descriptive statistics

The PRC’s assertiveness in the South China Sea intensified in some form
in 8 out of the 10 years in the 1970s, 9 out of 10 years in the 1980s, 9
out of 10 years in the 1990s, and every year between 2000 and 2015. Most
intensifications of PRC assertive activity continued in subsequent
years, establishing new baselines. Of the 132 cases in the events
dataset, 50 (37.8%) are likely to have continued through 2015, and a
further 23 (17.4%) continued at least into the following year. Only 55
(41.7%) were one-off incidents or temporary surges in activity that
lasted less than a year.

Dyadic and issue analysis

To identify variation in the targets and immediate purposes of the PRC’s
assertiveness, each intensified assertive action was coded according to
which bilateral relationships was implicated, and which of the key
issues of security, administrative control, resources and politics the
action addressed.

In 64 cases (48%) a single dyad was identifiable, usually due to one
particular state’s assets being targeted, or because the events in
question concerned the Paracel Islands and Scarborough Shoal, which are
bilateral PRC-Vietnam (coded V) and PRC-Philippines disputes (coded P)
respectively. Other activities concerned disputes over surveillance
activities and interpretations of international law between the PRC and
the US (coded U). Some 21 assertive actions (16%) have implications for
states at large — such as introduction of civil airspace regulations, or
military exercises taking place in unspecified waters within the sea.
Such cases were coded (A).

Events concerning the Spratly Islands present the most complexity in
identifying the bilateral relationships impacted by assertive actions.
Where an action took place in, or was targeted at, an identifiable
geographical area, this was considered to involve the dyads formed
between the PRC and other claimants to that area. For example,
activities to strengthen administration of the “central South China Sea”
area referred to in many PRC sources, concern areas between the Paracel
and Spratly Islands that touch upon the EEZ and continental shelf claims
of the Philippines as well as Vietnam. Such cases were thus coded as
both (P) and (V). The PRC’s outposts in the Spratly Islands are disputed
by Vietnam and the Philippines only, so actions taking place there were
likewise coded as (P) and (V), except where they had clear strategic
implications for other states, such as the large-scale island-building
campaign in that area from 2013 onwards. Where an action concerned PRC
claims to the Spratly Islands as a whole, as the PRC’s verbal assertions
typically do, this would implicate three bilateral relationships:
PRC-Malaysia (M), PRC-Philippines (P), and PRC-Vietnam (V). Finally,
where the PRC’s actions concerned the entire nine-dash line area, such
as surveys or patrols of the area, this also implied, in the case of
energy developments, PRC-Brunei relations (B) and, in the case of
fisheries, PRC-Indonesia relations (I).

Not surprisingly, given the larger scope of dispute between the two
countries the Sino-Vietnamese relationship was the most often impacted
by the PRC’s assertive conduct, with 71% of the cases involving
PRC-Vietnam disputes. China-Philippine disputes were the next most
commonly featured, with 52% of cases. PRC actions implicating its
relations with Malaysia comprised 36%, with Brunei 15%, and Indonesia
11%. Only 8% of assertive behavioral changes — 11 of the 132 cases — had
specific implications for Sino-US relations, while twenty-three cases,
or 17%, had implications for states in general.

Over time, as Table A4 shows, the PRC’s assertive advances have tended
to impinge on the interests of a broadening array of states. The PRC’s
surges in the 1970s, 1980s and 1990s were predominantly at the expense
of Vietnam and the Philippines, and to a lesser extent Malaysia and
Brunei. However, the post-2007 period has seen an increase in assertive
actions affecting the claims and interests of Indonesia, the United
States, and states in general (A).

Coding checks

The four types of assertive action that comprise the typology outlined
in the main text were designed to be clearly defined and mutually
exclusive. Despite these efforts, some ambiguities inevitably remain. To
check the analytic validity of the typology and the author’s coding
(C0), two students were employed to independently re-code the 132 cases
on the basis of the case narratives and the typology as described in the
article. One brought a background knowledge of the South China Sea
dispute (C1), while the other had previously studied architecture and
had recently commenced a Master’s degree in international affairs (C2).
One was an American citizen, the other a PRC citizen, and both were
enrolled at US universities. C1’s coding matched the author’s for 122
out of 132 cases (92.4%), while C2’s was identical for 115 cases
(87.1%). Both C1 and C2’s codings produce even larger differences in the
mean number of coercive cases before and after 2007. This should
increase confidence that the qualitative behavioral change identified in
2007 is the result of a qualitative shift in the PRC’s conduct, rather
than biases of the author.

[2] Given the importance of worst-case planning in state security
policies, the perception of security threats resulting from increased
assertiveness may outweigh the material benefits of any simultaneous
conciliatory actions. If so, it is not appropriate to implicitly equate
the two by offsetting assertiveness against conciliatory actions in the
same dataset. This is not to dismiss the significance of conciliatory
measures, but it does suggest they should be considered separately.
Assertiveness in maritime territorial disputes could be mitigated by
conciliatory and cooperative actions if they credibly suggest some
limitation on assertive actions in the future. Resource-sharing
arrangements, such as joint development of energy resources and
cooperative fisheries management, may help to ease rivals’ concerns
about missing out on disputed resources altogether. Joint patrols allow
two countries to simultaneously manifest administrative presence in a
disputed area without undermining each other’s claims, as does
cooperation over non-traditional security challenges affecting the area.
CBMs and crisis management mechanisms such as hotlines and on-water
protocols, if implemented effectively, can increase the security of all
states’ positions by rendering their rivals’ actions more predictable,
thereby decreasing the likelihood that an unplanned encounter will
result in a sudden severe negative shift in their relative bargaining
position. And multilateral documents pledging policy restraint, such as
the 2003 ASEAN-China DOC, can also mitigate assertiveness to the extent
that they generate belief in the likelihood of future assertive actions
diminishing or at least not intensifying. However, establishing how, and
indeed if, the two should to be balanced against each other, will
require significant further investigation.
