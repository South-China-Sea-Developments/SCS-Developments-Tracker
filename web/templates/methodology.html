Methodology

How the moves are coded

Andrew Chubb's coding rules (MATS), unchanged, carried forward from 2016 and extended to seven claimants; every move dated, placed and sourced.

The South China Sea Developments Tracker continues Andrew Chubb's Maritime Assertiveness Time Series (MATS). MATS 1.0 coded assertive moves by China, the Philippines and Vietnam in the South China Sea from 1970 to 2015. This project keeps that coding scheme unchanged, carries the series forward to the present, extends it to Brunei, Indonesia, Malaysia and Taiwan, and adds an event layer beneath each move with dates, locations and sources.

What counts as a move

A move is an intensification of a claimant's assertiveness compared with the previous calendar year, shown by one of three criteria: qualitative novelty, increased frequency, or expanded geographic scope. An action that merely continues the previous year's behaviour at the same level is not a new move; it is covered by the earlier move's estimated duration. Only non-cooperative behaviour is coded; conciliatory actions are deliberately excluded.

Where the data come from

The 1970–2015 moves for China, the Philippines and Vietnam are Chubb's MATS 1.02, loaded unchanged. The full methodology, including source lists, verification practice and inter-coder checks, is in the appendix to Chubb (2021), released under CC0 and kept as the project codebook in the repository.

Each claimant's 1970 level is read against a baseline of its known actions before 1970: Chubb's own list for China, and lists compiled by this project for the other five (BASELINE-*.md on the Downloads page), each row with its source.

From 2016 on, and for Brunei, Indonesia, Malaysia and Taiwan throughout, the moves are coded by the project with Chubb's rules, from official statements, parliamentary and government records, news reports and open-source research. AI tools help gather and summarise sources and suggest a first coding; the project's coder checks every candidate against its sources and decides what enters the dataset and how it is coded. Project-coded moves are labelled as such wherever they appear. How candidates are compiled and checked, and how often the suggested coding was kept, is set out in a short note (also on the Downloads page).

Every change to the dataset is a versioned commit in the public repository, and an automatic check keeps the 1970–2015 figures for China, the Philippines and Vietnam exactly as Chubb published them.

Reading the map

The map at the top of the tracker shades each of the ten sub-regions by the moves of a five-year window, the most recent first. Every move counts on each sub-region it is coded for, weighted by its most severe type: declarative 1, demonstrative 2, coercive 3, force 4. A sub-region's score is the sum of those weights in the window, for all claimants or for the one chosen. The scale is fixed across every window (shown on a square-root ramp, so lower scores stay visible beside the highest), so a darker shade in one period than in another means more moves, harsher moves, or both. A claim covering the whole sea counts on sub-regions 2 to 10 at once; by default the map shares each move's weight among the sub-regions it covers (a claim over nine of them adds a ninth of its weight to each), and a switch below the map counts every move in full on each instead. An arrow beside a sub-region's number marks a rise (▲) or fall (▼) of a fifth or more in its score against the five years before. The number on each sub-region is its count of moves in the window. Zoomed in, or with a sub-region chosen, the map shows the reefs, shoals and islands the moves' descriptions name, matched against the project's gazetteer; a move that names none (about two in three) is known only to its sub-regions. The types of move, the claimant and the party a move is directed at can be narrowed below the map; the timeline above the slider counts the moves per year for the same choice. A switch shows instead the build-up since 1970, every move up to the year chosen, as the original MAVD map does. The slider moves the window back in time; the Events page shows the events one by one, with filters.

A point is one event: a patrol intercept, a statement, a drilling start, dated and sourced on its move's page. A filled point has coordinates of its own; a hollow point stands at the named feature (reef, shoal, island) the sources mention, placed with the project's gazetteer. Every event is counted on a sub-region — its own if one is known, otherwise once on each sub-region of its move, so the numbers on the ten polygons can add up to more than the number of events — and those with coordinates are drawn at them as well; the sub-region counts therefore hold the whole dataset, whatever the sources could place. The filters use the move's coding (type, domain, issue, counterpart), because MATS variables belong to the move, not the event. The base map is drawn from OpenFreeMap vector tiles (OpenMapTiles schema, © OpenStreetMap contributors, ODbL), recoloured by the project, with English names in the Latin alphabet wherever the data has them; the tiles and their fonts are the only things on the tracker not in the bundle, and without them the sub-regions and events still draw. Boundaries and names on the base map are those of its providers; the feature names this project uses follow the source text and Wikidata's English labels; neither implies a position on any claim.

The claimant buttons below the map share one scale, the window's, so their shades compare across claimants. The Events page counts every event in the period chosen; most are known only to their sub-region, and those the sources place are drawn at a point as well. The review notes kept with each event (the source's wording for a place, a possible duplicate, whether an excerpt was checked against its page) are in the data files, not on the case pages.

The Issue menu keeps the map, its numbers and the claimant maps to the moves about one issue (military, administrative, energy, fisheries or politics): where each front runs. A move about two issues counts on both. A sub-region's panel shows what moved the score: its score in the five years shown and in the five before, split by claimant and by type, with the change in each, so an arrow can be traced to the claimants and kinds of move behind it. It shows which moves made up the change, not why they were made.

Lines on the map: EEZ boundaries, where settled or claimed, from Marine Regions (Maritime Boundaries v12, CC BY 4.0); China's dashed line, shown as a claim, from Natural Earth (public domain); who holds each feature, its outposts drawn in the holder's colour, from Wikipedia's list of Spratly Islands features (read 2026-11-23), the Paracels and Scarborough Shoal.

Between claimants

The Between claimants page shows how each claimant has directed its moves at the others. A move counts for a pair when its dyads name the other claimant: CV is a move by China directed at Vietnam. The measure is the homepage map’s: a five-year window (or the build-up since {{ meta.first_year }}: every year up to the one chosen, which the slider and Play move through), each move weighted by its most severe type (declarative 1, demonstrative 2, coercive 3, force 4), on a scale fixed across every window and shown on the same square-root ramp, with an arrow for a rise or fall of a fifth or more against the five years before. On the page of all pairs, a line’s width and shade are the pair’s score, both directions together; choosing a claimant turns the lines into arrows for one direction. Each pair has its own page, whose map shades the ten sub-regions by the pair’s score, sharing a move’s weight among the sub-regions it covers by default, as the homepage does.

Moves aimed at all claimants (dyad A), such as a law covering the whole sea, are counted in the figures and shown in grey, but left out of the share coded coercive or force and off the maps. Under ten moves no share is given. China and Taiwan are drawn apart and left out of the rankings, since Beijing treats Taipei’s claim as China’s own. A move is in force from its year to the last year it is recorded as ongoing; an upper bound counts every move whose end is still to be re-checked as still running. Because a move marks a step up against the year before, a standoff held at the same level shows in how long a move lasts rather than in new moves, and a lower count can reflect fewer public reports as well as fewer moves: each pair’s page says who coded its moves and in what languages its sources are.

Episodes

An episode follows an exchange between two claimants at one place. The pipeline proposes episodes from the dated events with coordinates: two events are linked when they belong to claimants whose moves are directed at each other, lie within 25 km of each other and fall within six months of each other; an episode is a chain of such links holding events by both sides. Each proposal is then confirmed, named or rejected by the maintainer, and only confirmed episodes are shown. Events dated only to the year, or placed only to a sub-region, cannot take part, so episodes are rarer in the earlier decades, where fewer events carry a position.

An episode shows a sequence. It does not suggest that one act caused another, and the gap of six months is a rule of thumb rather than a finding: two runs of events at the same place a little further apart are shown as two episodes. On an episode’s page the map shows where (one marker per place, its ring split between the two claimants’ events) and the strip shows when.
